You've probably seen it at the gym. Someone walks in with a giant gallon jug (about 3.8 L) and sips from it all day. Then a friend tells you eight glasses is the magic number. Then your phone app pings you every hour to drink more.

So how much water should I drink a day, really? If you've asked yourself that, you're in good company. Here's the good news: the real answer is simpler, and a lot more relaxed, than the gallon-jug crowd makes it sound.

Let me walk you through what the official numbers say, what counts toward them, and when you actually need more.

## How much water should I drink a day? The short answer

The best-known numbers come from the National Academies (the US science body that sets nutrient targets). In its report on water, it set an "adequate intake" of **total water**:

- **Men:** about 3.7 liters (125 oz) a day
- **Women:** about 2.7 liters (91 oz) a day

"Total water" is the key phrase. It means water from everything: plain water, other drinks, and food. The report found that about 20% of the water people get comes from food. The other 80% comes from drinks.

So when I run the math the same way our [water intake calculator](/water-intake-calculator) does, drinks work out to roughly:

- **Men:** about 3.0 L (101 oz, or about 12.7 cups) a day
- **Women:** about 2.2 L (74 oz, or about 9.3 cups) a day

That's a lot less scary than a gallon.

### What "adequate intake" means

An adequate intake is not a strict minimum. The National Academies based these numbers on what people who appear well hydrated actually take in each day. Think of it as a helpful reference point, not a test you pass or fail.

## Different experts, different numbers

This is where it gets interesting. Europe's food safety agency, EFSA, looked at the same question in 2010 and landed on lower totals. Here's how the main guidelines compare:

| Guideline | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| National Academies (US), total water | 3.7 L (125 oz) | 2.7 L (91 oz) |
| National Academies, from drinks (about 80%) | about 3.0 L (101 oz) | about 2.2 L (74 oz) |
| EFSA (Europe), total water | 2.5 L (85 oz) | 2.0 L (68 oz) |
| EFSA, from drinks (about 80%) | about 2.0 L (68 oz) | about 1.6 L (54 oz) |
| NHS (UK), fluids | 6 to 8 cups or glasses | 6 to 8 cups or glasses |

The drink numbers in the table use the same 80% share from the National Academies report. EFSA's own split may differ a little.

What I take from this: nobody agrees on one exact number. But the ranges overlap, and every group counts food and other drinks. You have more wiggle room than you think.

## What about the "8 glasses a day" rule?

You've heard it a hundred times. Eight 8-ounce glasses, or 64 oz (about 1.9 L), every single day.

A 2002 review by Dr. Heinz Valtin, a physiologist at Dartmouth Medical School, went looking for the science behind this rule. He found no scientific studies that supported "8 x 8" for healthy adults in a mild climate who mostly sit during the day. Surveys of thousands of adults suggested such large amounts weren't needed for that group.

He was also clear about the flip side. More fluid makes sense during hard work or exercise, especially in hot weather.

The NHS still suggests 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day, but calls it "just a guide." That's a fair way to see it.

## Let thirst and your pee guide you

Here's the part most people find freeing. The National Academies report says the vast majority of healthy people meet their daily needs by letting thirst be their guide.

The NHS adds a simple check: aim to drink enough so your pee is a **clear, pale yellow**.

So you don't need to track every sip. If you drink when you're thirsty and your pee looks pale, you're very likely on track.

## What counts toward your daily water

This part is actually really easy, because more counts than you'd expect.

- **Coffee and tea count.** The National Academies found that caffeinated drinks can count toward your needs just like other drinks.
- **Milk and sugar-free drinks count.** The NHS lists water, lower-fat milk, and sugar-free drinks, including tea and coffee, as part of your daily intake.
- **Food counts.** Around 20% of your water comes from what you eat.

That said, plain water has a real edge. The CDC points out that water has no calories, so swapping sugary drinks for water can help cut calories. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025 to 2030 also say to choose water (still or sparkling) and unsweetened drinks.

If your drinks come with a lot of sugar, it's worth a look. Our [sugar intake calculator](/sugar-intake-calculator) shows how much fits in your day.

## When you need more water

The reference numbers are a starting point. Several things push your needs up.

The NHS says you may need more fluids if you are:

- pregnant or breastfeeding
- in a hot environment
- physically active for long periods
- ill or getting over an illness

The CDC also lists fever, diarrhea, and vomiting as reasons your body needs more water.

### For gym-goers: weigh yourself before and after

If you train hard, this is the simplest trick I know of, and it comes straight from the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM).

The ACSM position stand says the goal of drinking during exercise is to avoid losing more than **2% of your body weight** from water loss. Sweat rates vary a lot from person to person. So the ACSM recommends a custom plan, and you can estimate your own sweat rate by weighing yourself before and after exercise.

Here's how that works for a 170 lb (77 kg) person:

1. 2% of 170 lb is 3.4 lb (about 1.5 kg). That's the loss to stay under.
2. Weigh yourself right before a workout.
3. Weigh yourself right after, before you eat or drink.
4. Each pound you lost is roughly 15 oz (about 450 mL) of fluid to replace.

Try it once on a normal training day. Once you do this once, it becomes second nature, and you'll know your own number.

The ACSM also notes that drinks with electrolytes and carbs can help more than water alone in some situations. The 2025 to 2030 Dietary Guidelines add that very active people may benefit from more sodium to make up for sweat losses.

## Signs you may need to drink more

Your body gives you clues. According to the CDC, not getting enough water can lead to:

- unclear thinking
- mood changes
- overheating
- constipation
- kidney stones

Pee that is darker than pale yellow is another easy signal, based on the NHS guidance. If you notice these, reach for a glass of water first.

<Callout title="When to check with a professional">
If you have kidney disease, heart problems, or take medicines that affect fluids, your needs may be different. Talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian about the right amount for you. The same goes if you are pregnant or breastfeeding.
</Callout>

## Easy ways to drink more water

You don't need a gallon jug. Small habits work better. Here are tips from the CDC and NHS:

- **Carry a reusable bottle.** It's the easiest win of all.
- **Drink regularly through the day.** Don't try to catch up all at once at night.
- **Pick water over sugary drinks** when you can.
- **Add flavor.** A slice of lemon or lime, or sparkling water, makes it more fun.
- **Drink extra when you sweat.** Water is the best way to replace lost fluid, says the NHS.

A simple routine I like: a glass when you wake up, one with each meal, and a bottle during workouts. That covers a big chunk of your day without much thought.

## What this means for you

Let me pull it all together.

For most healthy adults, total water lands somewhere between the EFSA and National Academies numbers. That's 2.0 to 2.7 L (68 to 91 oz) for women and 2.5 to 3.7 L (85 to 125 oz) for men, counting food and all drinks.

Based on what I've seen in the research, the most useful habit isn't counting ounces. It's paying attention. Drink when you're thirsty, check your pee color, and drink more when it's hot or you're sweating.

## The bottom line

So, how much water should I drink a day? For most people, it's about 9.3 cups (2.2 L) of drinks for women and about 12.7 cups (3.0 L) for men, with food covering the rest. You don't need to force down a gallon.
