You pour a cup of cooked rice, check the label, and see about 45 grams of carbs. Is that a lot? A little? It is hard to say until you know how many carbs per day you actually need. Here's the good news. There is a clear, official range, and the math to find your own number takes about a minute.

Let me explain where the numbers come from, what they look like for real people, and how to make your carbs work harder for you.

## How many carbs per day? The official range

In 2005, the Institute of Medicine (now part of the National Academies) set two carb guidelines for adults.

- **The AMDR:** 45% to 65% of your daily calories from carbohydrate. AMDR stands for Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range. In plain words, it is the healthy range for how much of your plate comes from each macro.
- **The RDA:** 130 grams of carbohydrate a day. RDA means Recommended Dietary Allowance, the amount that covers the needs of nearly all healthy adults.

Here's the thing. The 130 g figure is a baseline, not a target. Most people eat well above it, and the percentage range is what shapes a normal day.

Each gram of carbohydrate has 4 calories. So to turn the percentage into grams, you multiply your calories by 0.45 and 0.65, then divide by 4.

| Daily calories | 45% of calories | 65% of calories |
|---|---|---|
| 1,500 | 169 g | 244 g |
| 1,800 | 203 g | 293 g |
| 2,000 | 225 g | 325 g |
| 2,500 | 281 g | 406 g |
| 3,000 | 338 g | 488 g |

That is a wide range. The rest of this guide helps you figure out where in that range you fit.

## Step one: know your calories

Your carb target depends on your calorie target. Your calorie target depends on your size, age, sex, and how active you are.

Our [TDEE calculator](/tdee-calculator) uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. A 2005 review found it was the most likely of the common equations to land within 10% of measured resting calories in healthy adults. It then multiplies by an activity factor, from 1.2 for a desk job to 1.9 for hard daily training.

Once you know your calories, you can do the carb math by hand or let our [carb calculator](/carb-calculator) do it for you.

## Carbs per day for women, men, and gym-goers

Numbers make more sense with real examples. I ran three sample people through the same Mifflin-St Jeor formula our calculators use. These are illustrations, not advice for any one person.

### A woman, 150 lb (68 kg), 5 ft 5 in (165 cm), age 35, lightly active

Her maintenance calories come out to about **1,890 a day**.

- 45% to 65% of 1,890 calories = about **213 to 307 g of carbs a day**.
- If she wants to lose weight, our TDEE calculator's weight-loss setting takes off 20%, giving about 1,510 calories. That makes her range about **170 to 245 g**.

### A man, 190 lb (86 kg), 5 ft 10 in (178 cm), age 40, moderately active

His maintenance calories come out to about **2,760 a day**.

- 45% to 65% of 2,760 calories = about **311 to 449 g of carbs a day**.

That upper number looks huge, and that's fine. It is a ceiling, not a goal. Where he lands depends on how much protein and fat he eats.

### A gym-goer, 180 lb (82 kg), 5 ft 10 in (178 cm), age 28, trains 3 to 5 days a week

Our protein calculator gave him a muscle-building target of about 3,060 calories. It sets protein first (155 g, the middle of his range), puts fat at 30% of calories, and gives carbs the rest.

That works out to about **381 g of carbs a day**, or 50% of his calories. This is where it gets interesting. His carb percentage sits right in the middle of the official range, but his grams are high because he eats a lot of calories. Percent stays steady. Grams grow with your appetite.

You can try this split yourself with our [macro calculator](/macro-calculator). It lets you pick 20%, 30%, or 35% fat, all within the healthy 20% to 35% fat range from the same Institute of Medicine report.

## Carb quality matters as much as carb grams

Two people can eat 250 g of carbs and have very different days. One gets beans, oats, fruit, and potatoes. The other gets soda and pastries. The grams match, but the food does not.

Here are two simple checks to steer your carbs in the right direction.

### 1. Get enough fiber

Fiber is a type of carbohydrate your body can't fully digest. It lives in beans, whole grains, fruit, vegetables, nuts, and seeds.

The Institute of Medicine suggests **14 g of fiber per 1,000 calories**. For adults, the daily adequate intake is:

- Women 19 to 50: 25 g. Women 51+: 21 g.
- Men 19 to 50: 38 g. Men 51+: 30 g.

So the 1,890-calorie woman above would aim for about 26 g of fiber a day.

### 2. Keep added sugar low

The American Heart Association suggests no more than about **25 g (6 teaspoons) of added sugar a day for most women** and **36 g (9 teaspoons) for most men**. The World Health Organization says to keep free sugars under 10% of calories, with extra benefits below 5%.

Added sugar still counts toward your carb total. It just brings fewer nutrients along with it.

## How many carbs are in common foods?

This is where a daily target starts to feel real. Here are carb counts for ordinary portions, based on USDA FoodData Central.

| Food | Portion | Carbs | Fiber |
|---|---|---|---|
| White rice, cooked | 1 cup (158 g) | 45 g | 0.6 g |
| Lentils, cooked | 1 cup (198 g) | 40 g | 16 g |
| Baked potato with skin | 1 medium (173 g) | 37 g | 4 g |
| Oats, dry | ½ cup (40 g) | 27 g | 4 g |
| Banana | 1 medium (118 g) | 27 g | 3 g |
| Apple with skin | 1 medium (182 g) | 25 g | 4 g |
| Roti or chapati | 1 piece (40 g) | 19 g | 2 g |
| Whole-wheat bread | 1 slice (32 g) | 14 g | 2 g |
| 2% milk | 1 cup (8 fl oz, 244 g) | 12 g | 0 g |
| Broccoli, cooked | 1 cup (156 g) | 11 g | 5 g |

Look at lentils. They carry almost as many carbs as rice, but about 25 times the fiber. They also bring protein. Swaps like that make the same carb budget go further.

## Eating out without guessing

Restaurant meals can hide a lot of carbs, or surprisingly few. A couple of examples from the chain data on our site:

- **Chipotle:** the Double High Protein Bowl has 54 g of carbs with 81 g of protein. The High Protein - Low Calorie Bowl has just 18 g of carbs and 35 g of protein.
- **Subway:** a 6-inch Grilled Chicken sub has 43 g of carbs. The Grilled Chicken Protein Bowl has 12 g.

Based on what I've seen in chain menus, bread, tortillas, and rice drive most of the carb count. Swap the base and the number drops fast.

## Can you go lower than 45%?

Yes, many people do. Lower-carb eating is a valid choice for some people, and very low-carb plans like keto go under 50 g a day on purpose. If that interests you, our guide on [how many carbs on keto](/guides/how-many-carbs-on-keto) walks through it.

Just know that fiber lives mostly in carb foods like beans, whole grains, and fruit. Cutting them hard makes the fiber targets above tougher to hit, so plan for it.

<Callout title="When to get personal advice">
If you have diabetes or prediabetes, kidney disease, a history of an eating disorder, or you are pregnant or breastfeeding, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before changing your carb intake. The same goes if you take medicines that affect blood sugar.
</Callout>

## What this means for you

Here is the simple version.

1. Find your daily calories.
2. Multiply by 0.45 and 0.65, then divide by 4. That is your carb range in grams.
3. Pick a spot in that range that fits how you like to eat and how active you are.
4. Fill it mostly with high-fiber foods, and keep added sugar low.

My honest take? Most people don't need to track carbs to the gram. Knowing your rough range, plus a few food anchors like "a cup of rice is about 45 g," gets you most of the way.

## The bottom line on how many carbs per day

So, how many carbs per day do you need? For most adults, 45% to 65% of calories is the healthy range, which is 225 to 325 g on a 2,000-calorie day. Your own number moves with your calories, your training, and your goals.

Start with your calories, run the numbers in our [carb calculator](/carb-calculator), and then focus on quality. Once you know your range, reading a food label gets a whole lot easier.
