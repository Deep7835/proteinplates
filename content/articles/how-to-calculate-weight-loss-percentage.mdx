Say you step on the scale and see you're down 12 pounds. That feels great. Then a friend mentions she lost 12 pounds too, but she started 60 pounds lighter than you. Did you both make the same progress? Not quite. That's why your weight loss percentage tells a clearer story than pounds alone.

Here's the good news. The math is simple, and you only need two numbers. By the end of this guide, you'll be able to do it in your head.

## What weight loss percentage means

Your weight loss percentage is the share of your starting weight that you've lost. It turns "I lost 12 pounds" into "I lost 6% of my body weight" (if you started at 200 lb, or 91 kg).

Why bother? Because percentage scales to your size. Look at the same 10 lb (4.5 kg) loss from three different starting points:

| Starting weight | Lost | Weight loss percentage |
|---|---|---|
| 150 lb (68 kg) | 10 lb (4.5 kg) | 6.7% |
| 200 lb (91 kg) | 10 lb (4.5 kg) | 5.0% |
| 250 lb (113 kg) | 10 lb (4.5 kg) | 4.0% |

Same pounds, very different progress. Health agencies like the CDC and NIH also talk about weight loss in percentages, so this number helps you compare your progress to their guidance.

## How to calculate weight loss percentage in 3 steps

This part is actually really easy. Grab your starting weight and your current weight.

1. **Subtract** your current weight from your starting weight. That's how much you've lost.
2. **Divide** that number by your starting weight.
3. **Multiply** by 100. That's your percentage.

As a formula:

**Weight loss % = (starting weight minus current weight) ÷ starting weight × 100**

### Example in pounds

You started at 200 lb and now weigh 186 lb.

1. 200 minus 186 = 14 lb lost
2. 14 ÷ 200 = 0.07
3. 0.07 × 100 = **7%**

### Example in kilograms

You started at 80 kg (176 lb) and now weigh 76 kg (168 lb).

1. 80 minus 76 = 4 kg lost
2. 4 ÷ 80 = 0.05
3. 0.05 × 100 = **5%**

The formula works in any unit. Just make sure both weights use the same one. Don't mix pounds and kilograms in the same sum.

If the answer comes out negative, that means your weight went up, not down. And if you'd rather skip the math, our [weight loss percentage calculator](/weight-loss-percentage-calculator) does it in a second.

## How to find your goal weight for a set percentage

You can also run the math backward. This is handy for setting a goal.

**Goal weight = starting weight × (1 minus your percentage as a decimal)**

For a 5% goal, multiply by 0.95. For 10%, multiply by 0.90.

| Starting weight | 5% goal | 10% goal |
|---|---|---|
| 150 lb (68 kg) | 142.5 lb (64.6 kg) | 135 lb (61.2 kg) |
| 200 lb (91 kg) | 190 lb (86.2 kg) | 180 lb (81.6 kg) |
| 250 lb (113 kg) | 237.5 lb (107.7 kg) | 225 lb (102.1 kg) |
| 300 lb (136 kg) | 285 lb (129.3 kg) | 270 lb (122.5 kg) |

Seeing a smaller, clear goal like this can make a big goal feel a lot more doable.

## Why 5% is a milestone worth celebrating

This is where the numbers get encouraging. You don't have to lose a huge amount to see health benefits.

- **The CDC** says a 5% weight loss, which is 10 pounds for someone who weighs 200 pounds, could lower the risk for some chronic diseases, such as heart disease, prediabetes, and type 2 diabetes.
- **The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI)** says health professionals recommend losing 5% to 10% of your starting weight over about 6 months. It adds that even 3% to 5% can lower blood triglycerides and blood sugar, and the risk of type 2 diabetes. Losing more than 3% to 5% can improve blood pressure and cholesterol.
- **The NIDDK** says that people at risk for type 2 diabetes may be able to prevent or delay it by losing at least 5% to 7% of their starting weight. For a 200 lb (91 kg) person, that is about 10 to 14 lb (4.5 to 6.4 kg).

### A note for women

The NIDDK also notes that women with obesity who lose 5% of their body weight may increase their chances of having regular periods, ovulating, and becoming pregnant. If this applies to you, your doctor is the best person to talk through a plan with.

## How fast should the percentage go up?

The CDC says people who lose weight gradually, about 1 to 2 pounds (0.5 to 0.9 kg) a week, are more likely to keep it off.

In percentage terms, for a 200 lb (91 kg) person, that's about 0.5% to 1% of body weight a week. For a 150 lb (68 kg) person, it's about 0.7% to 1.3% a week.

### Why your progress slows down

Here's something many people find frustrating, so let me explain it. Weight loss usually slows over time, even if you keep eating the same way.

A 2011 modeling study in The Lancet, led by Kevin Hall at the NIH, found that body weight responds slowly to a change in calories. It takes about a year to get halfway to your new stable weight. That's also why the old rule that "3,500 calories equals 1 pound" overestimates long-term weight loss.

What this means for you: a slower month later on is normal. It doesn't mean you did something wrong. The NIH's free [Body Weight Planner](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/bwp) is built on Hall's model if you'd like a more realistic forecast.

## Tracking weight loss percentage on a GLP-1 medicine

The math is exactly the same if you're taking a GLP-1 medicine like Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, or Zepbound. Your care team may track this number too, and it's a handy way to talk about progress with them.

One thing the scale can't tell you is what kind of weight you lost. The 2025 joint advisory on nutrition with GLP-1 medicines lists muscle and bone loss as key concerns. It recommends resistance training and enough protein, 1.2 to 1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight a day during active weight loss.

So alongside your weight loss percentage, keep an eye on your protein and your strength. Our guide on [protein on Ozempic](/guides/protein-on-ozempic) covers easy protein-first meals. If you want to track body composition too, our [body fat calculator](/body-fat-calculator) is a good companion.

## Tips for tracking your weight loss percentage

A few simple habits make your numbers more reliable.

- **Weigh the same way each time.** Same scale, same time of day, similar clothes.
- **Record your true starting weight.** Your percentage is only as good as that first number.
- **Look at the trend.** Check your percentage every 2 to 4 weeks, not every morning.
- **Pair it with a plan.** Our [calorie deficit calculator](/calorie-deficit-calculator) can help you set a daily calorie target that fits a steady pace.

<Callout title="When to talk to a professional">
If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, have a history of an eating disorder, have diabetes or kidney disease, or take medicines, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before setting a weight loss goal. They can help you choose a safe target.
</Callout>

## The bottom line on weight loss percentage

To find your weight loss percentage, subtract your current weight from your starting weight, divide by your starting weight, and multiply by 100. That's it.

This one number puts your progress in context. It lets you compare fairly, set clear goals, and see when you've hit meaningful milestones like 5%. According to the CDC and NIH, that first 5% can matter for your health.
