### What is the ideal weight for my height?

The ideal weight for your height is a range, not one number. The CDC calls a BMI of 18.5 to under 25 a healthy weight for adults. For someone who is 5 ft 6 in (168 cm), that works out to about 115 to 154 lb (52.0 to 70.0 kg). Formulas like Devine give a single midpoint.

Picture a woman in a doctor's waiting room, staring at an old poster that lists one "ideal weight" for every height. For her 5 ft 5 in (165 cm), it says 126 lb (57 kg). She weighs 140 lb (64 kg) and feels strong. So which number is right? Knowing where these numbers come from makes them far less stressful.

## Where do ideal weight numbers come from?

Most ideal weight charts use one of two ideas: the BMI range or a single-number formula.

**1. The BMI range.** BMI (body mass index) is your weight in kilograms divided by your height in meters squared. The CDC calls a BMI of 18.5 to under 25 a healthy weight for adults. Flip the math around, and you can find the weight range that fits your height.

**2. Ideal body weight formulas.** These give one number for each height. The best known is the Devine formula from 1974. Others include Robinson (1983), Miller (1983), and Hamwi (1964).

This is where it gets interesting. A 2000 review by Pai and Paloucek in the *Annals of Pharmacotherapy* traced these formulas back to their roots. They were built to help doctors dose medicines. They were never meant to be personal health goals. They're useful reference points, just not a finish line.

### How is the BMI column worked out?

Multiply the BMI by your height in meters, twice. This part is actually really easy:

**Weight (kg) = BMI × height (m) × height (m)**

Take 5 ft 6 in as an example. That's 66 inches, or 1.676 m. Squared, it's about 2.81.

- Low end: 18.5 × 2.81 = 52.0 kg (115 lb)
- High end: 24.9 × 2.81 = 70.0 kg (154 lb)

To turn kilograms into pounds, multiply by 2.2046.

### How are the Devine columns worked out?

The Devine formula adds 2.3 kg for every inch over 5 feet:

- **Women:** 45.5 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 ft
- **Men:** 50 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 ft

For a woman who is 5 ft 6 in, that's 45.5 + (2.3 × 6) = 59.3 kg, or about 131 lb.

## Do the ideal weight formulas agree?

Roughly, but not exactly. At the same height, the four formulas spread out by 7 to 10 lb (3 to 5 kg). Here are two example heights run through all four:

| Formula | Woman, 5 ft 5 in (165 cm) | Man, 5 ft 10 in (178 cm) |
|---|---|---|
| Devine (1974) | 126 lb (57.0 kg) | 161 lb (73.0 kg) |
| Robinson (1983) | 127 lb (57.5 kg) | 157 lb (71.0 kg) |
| Miller (1983) | 132 lb (59.9 kg) | 155 lb (70.3 kg) |
| Hamwi (1964) | 125 lb (56.5 kg) | 165 lb (75.0 kg) |

What stands out to me: every one of them sits well inside the healthy BMI range. That's a good reminder that "ideal" is really a zone, not a point.

Remember the woman in the waiting room? At 5 ft 5 in, 126 lb is the Devine number. Her 140 lb (64 kg) is still well inside the healthy BMI range for her height, which runs from about 111 to 150 lb (50.4 to 67.9 kg).

## Is a weight chart enough to judge your health?

No. A weight chart is a helpful first look, but weight alone can't tell muscle from fat. The CDC says BMI is a screening measure and should be looked at along with other factors.

Two easy extras add real detail:

- **Your waist.** NICE, the health guidance body in England, suggests keeping your waist to less than half your height. Check yours with our [waist-to-height ratio calculator](/waist-to-height-ratio-calculator).
- **Your body fat.** Two people at the same weight can have very different body fat. Our [body fat calculator](/body-fat-calculator) gives you an estimate with just a tape measure.

### What should women keep in mind?

You don't have to chase the low end of the chart. Many women do. But the whole range from 18.5 to 24.9 counts as healthy weight in the CDC categories. If a number on the scale is causing stress, or you have a history of disordered eating, please talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian.

### What should men keep in mind?

Muscle can push you above the chart without adding fat. If you lift weights, you may sit above the Devine number or even above a BMI of 25 while carrying little fat. That's where a waist or body fat check helps. NICE notes that waist-to-height ratio can be used for people with high muscle mass.

### What should adults over 50 keep in mind?

The math stays the same, but muscle matters more. The CDC's adult BMI categories apply to adults 20 and older, so the chart doesn't change with age.

The PROT-AGE Study Group, which focused on adults over 65, suggests 1.0 to 1.2 g of protein per kg of body weight a day for older adults. For a 150 lb (68 kg) person, that's about 68 to 82 g a day. Our guide to [protein after 50](/guides/protein-after-50) walks you through it. If you have kidney disease or another health condition, check with your doctor before changing your protein.

## How should you use an ideal weight chart?

Use it as a map, not a verdict. That's my take, based on what I've seen. Find your height. Look at the whole range. Then add one or two other checks, like your waist size.

I'd also trust single-number formulas less than old posters do. They were made for dosing medicine. They're fine as a reference, and it's genuinely useful to know where they came from.

## So, what is your ideal weight for height?

It's a range, not one number. For most adults, the CDC healthy BMI range of 18.5 to under 25 is the best place to start, and the Devine formula gives a handy midpoint.
