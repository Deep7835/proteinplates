## What is BMR?

BMR stands for **basal metabolic rate**. It is the number of calories your body burns in a day at complete rest. Think of it as the cost of keeping the lights on: breathing, pumping blood, keeping your brain working, and holding your body temperature steady.

You will also see the term **RMR** (resting metabolic rate) or REE (resting energy expenditure). These are close cousins of BMR, and the research papers behind most calculators actually measured resting burn. For everyday use, you can treat them as the same idea.

How do scientists measure it? With a lab test called **indirect calorimetry**. You lie still while a machine measures the oxygen you breathe in and the carbon dioxide you breathe out. From that, researchers work out how much energy you are burning.

Most of us will never do that test. So we use formulas instead.

## BMR vs TDEE: what is the difference?

This is where a lot of people get mixed up.

- **BMR** is your burn at rest.
- **TDEE** (total daily energy expenditure) is your BMR plus everything you do: walking, working, training, even fidgeting.

To go from BMR to TDEE, you multiply by an activity factor. Our calculators use 1.2 (sedentary), 1.375 (lightly active), 1.55 (moderately active), 1.725 (very active), or 1.9 (extremely active). Your TDEE is your **maintenance calories**, the amount that keeps your weight steady. Our [TDEE calculator](/tdee-calculator) does this step for you.

## How BMR is calculated: 3 common formulas

There are several BMR formulas. Here are the three you will see most often. Use weight in kilograms (1 lb = 0.4536 kg), height in centimeters (1 inch = 2.54 cm), and age in years.

### 1. Mifflin-St Jeor (1990)

- **Women:** (10 × weight) + (6.25 × height) - (5 × age) - 161
- **Men:** (10 × weight) + (6.25 × height) - (5 × age) + 5

Researchers at the University of Nevada built this from lab measurements of 498 healthy adults aged 19 to 78, both with and without obesity. A 2005 systematic review by Frankenfield and colleagues for the American Dietetic Association found it was the most reliable of the common equations. It predicted resting burn within 10% of the measured value in more people than any other equation tested. That is why our calculators use it by default.

### 2. Revised Harris-Benedict (1984)

- **Women:** 447.593 + (9.247 × weight) + (3.098 × height) - (4.330 × age)
- **Men:** 88.362 + (13.397 × weight) + (4.799 × height) - (5.677 × age)

The original Harris-Benedict equation dates back to 1919. Roza and Shizgal updated it in 1984 and reported it estimates resting burn in healthy people with a precision of about 14%. In the 1990 Mifflin study, the original 1919 version overestimated measured resting burn by 5%.

### 3. Katch-McArdle

- **Everyone:** 370 + (21.6 × lean body mass in kg)

This one skips weight, height, age, and sex. It only uses your **lean body mass**, meaning everything that is not fat: muscle, bone, organs, and water. You need to know your body fat percentage to use it.

## A worked example: one woman, three formulas

When I ran a 30-year-old woman who is 5 ft 6 in (167.6 cm), weighs 140 lb (63.5 kg), and has 28% body fat through all three formulas, here is what came out.

**Mifflin-St Jeor:**
- 10 × 63.5 = 635
- 6.25 × 167.6 = 1,047.5
- 5 × 30 = 150
- 635 + 1,047.5 - 150 - 161 = **about 1,372 calories**

**Revised Harris-Benedict:**
- 9.247 × 63.5 = 587.2
- 3.098 × 167.6 = 519.2
- 4.330 × 30 = 129.9
- 447.6 + 587.2 + 519.2 - 129.9 = **about 1,424 calories**

**Katch-McArdle:**
- Lean mass = 63.5 × (1 - 0.28) = 45.7 kg (100.8 lb)
- 370 + (21.6 × 45.7) = **about 1,357 calories**

The three answers sit within about 70 calories of each other. For her, the formulas mostly agree.

## Why lean mass matters (especially if you lift)

This is genuinely fascinating. In the original 1990 Mifflin study, **fat-free mass was the best single predictor of resting burn**, even better than body weight alone. In plain words, what your body is made of matters, not just how much it weighs.

Here is how that plays out for a lifter. Take a 28-year-old man who is 5 ft 10 in (177.8 cm), weighs 180 lb (81.6 kg), and has 15% body fat:

| Formula | Estimated BMR |
|---|---|
| Mifflin-St Jeor | about 1,792 calories |
| Revised Harris-Benedict | about 1,876 calories |
| Katch-McArdle (lean mass 69.4 kg, or 153 lb) | about 1,868 calories |

For him, the lean-mass formula lands about 76 calories higher than Mifflin-St Jeor. If you train hard and know your body fat percentage fairly well, Katch-McArdle may suit you better. One honest caveat: Katch-McArdle is only as good as your body fat number. A rough guess can throw it off.

## What changes your BMR?

Look at the formulas and you can see what they treat as important:

- **Weight:** a bigger body costs more to run.
- **Height:** taller people get a higher estimate at the same weight.
- **Age:** in Mifflin-St Jeor, each extra year lowers the estimate by 5 calories.
- **Sex:** at the same weight, height, and age, the Mifflin-St Jeor estimate for men is 166 calories higher than for women. That is the gap between the +5 and the -161.
- **Lean mass:** as the Mifflin study found, fat-free mass tracks closely with resting burn.

## The limits of any BMR formula

Here's why this actually matters to you. Every formula gives an estimate for an average person with your stats. You are not an average.

The Frankenfield review said this clearly: even the best equation has noteworthy errors for individuals. It also pointed out that older adults and US ethnic minorities were underrepresented in the research behind these equations. So treat your BMR as a smart starting point, not a fact carved in stone.

If you have a health condition or take medicines that you think may affect your weight, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian. They can tell you whether a lab measurement makes sense for you.
