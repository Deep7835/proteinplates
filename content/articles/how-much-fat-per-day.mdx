### How much fat per day should you eat?

Most adults should get 20% to 35% of their daily calories from fat, according to the Institute of Medicine. On a 2,000-calorie day, that is about 44 to 78 grams. The WHO and US Dietary Guidelines both say to keep saturated fat to no more than 10% of calories.

For years, fat was the bad guy, and shelves filled up with low-fat cookies. Then the story flipped, and now some people add butter to their coffee. Here's the thing: fat isn't good or bad on its own. You need some, and the amount and type are what matter.

## What is the recommended fat range?

The main US reference is 20% to 35% of your daily calories. It comes from the Institute of Medicine's 2005 report on nutrients, which calls it an "acceptable range" (the AMDR).

The World Health Organization uses a similar range. It says adults should get at least 15% of their calories from fat. It also says keeping total fat to 30% or less may help prevent unhealthy weight gain.

### Why do fat grams add up so fast?

Each gram of fat has **9 calories**, the NHS explains. A gram of protein or carbs has 4. So a small amount of fat carries a lot of energy.

That's not a problem. It just means portions matter.

## How many grams of fat is that for you?

Multiply your calories by the percentage, then divide by 9. Here's what that looks like:

| Daily calories | 20% fat | 30% fat | 35% fat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,600 | 36 g | 53 g | 62 g |
| 2,000 | 44 g | 67 g | 78 g |
| 2,500 | 56 g | 83 g | 97 g |
| 3,000 | 67 g | 100 g | 117 g |

Here are two sample people. Both were run through the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which estimates the calories you burn at rest, then adjusted for activity:

- **A 40-year-old woman**, 170 lb (77 kg), 5 ft 5 in (165 cm), lightly active, needs about 1,980 calories. Her fat range is about 44 to 77 g a day.
- **A 40-year-old man**, 190 lb (86 kg), 5 ft 10 in (178 cm), moderately active, needs about 2,760 calories. His fat range is about 61 to 107 g a day.

## How much saturated fat per day is okay?

Keep saturated fat to no more than 10% of your calories. The NHS puts it in grams: 30 g a day for men and 20 g for women.

Saturated fat is found mostly in animal foods, like fatty meat, butter, cheese, and cream. It's also in palm oil and coconut oil. Here's what the main guidelines say:

| Source | Saturated fat limit |
|---|---|
| World Health Organization | No more than 10% of calories |
| Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025 to 2030 | No more than 10% of calories |
| NHS (UK) | Men: 30 g a day. Women: 20 g a day |

At 2,000 calories, 10% works out to about 22 g of saturated fat. For the woman in our example, it's about 22 g. For the man, it's about 31 g.

Why the limit? The NHS says too much saturated fat can raise "bad" LDL cholesterol in your blood. That can increase the risk of heart disease and stroke.

### What about trans fat?

Keep it very low. Trans fat can also raise cholesterol, says the NHS.

The WHO recommends no more than 1% of calories from trans fat. The NHS sets a limit of about 5 g a day for adults. According to the WHO, industrially made trans fat is found in some baked and fried foods and packaged snacks.

## What are the different types of fat?

There are three main types: saturated, monounsaturated, and polyunsaturated. Most fats and oils are a mix of them, says the NHS.

- **Saturated fat:** fatty cuts of meat, sausages, butter, ghee, cheese, cream, pastries, palm oil, and coconut oil.
- **Monounsaturated fat:** olive oil, rapeseed (canola) oil, avocados, and some nuts like almonds and peanuts.
- **Polyunsaturated fat:** this includes omega-3 fats in oily fish like salmon, mackerel, and sardines. It also includes omega-6 fats in oils like sunflower and corn, plus walnuts.

The NHS says there's good evidence that replacing saturated fat with some unsaturated fat can help lower cholesterol. It also recommends at least 2 portions of fish a week, with 1 of them oily.

### Why do you need fat at all?

Fat isn't just calories. According to the WHO, two fatty acids (linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid) can only come from your diet. The NHS adds that fat helps your body absorb vitamins A, D, and E.

So cutting fat to near zero isn't the goal. Choosing it well is.

## How much fat is in common foods?

A single tablespoon of canola oil has 14.0 g of fat. Here's how much fat is in some everyday foods, from USDA data:

| Food | Portion | Total fat | Saturated fat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Canola oil | 1 tbsp | 14.0 g | 1.0 g |
| Olive oil | 1 tbsp | 13.5 g | 1.9 g |
| Ground beef, 90% lean, broiled | 3 oz (85 g) | 10.0 g | 3.9 g |
| Coho salmon, cooked | 3 oz (85 g) | 6.4 g | 1.4 g |
| Butter, salted | 1 pat | 4.1 g | 2.6 g |

USDA data also lists a large egg at about 4.8 g of fat.

What I find interesting is the oil. One tablespoon has almost as much fat as two servings of salmon. That's why cooking oil is worth measuring now and then.

## How much fat is in restaurant meals?

It swings a lot, from a few grams to more than a whole day's worth. A few examples from our chain guides:

- **Chick-fil-A:** 8 ct Grilled Nuggets have just 3 g of fat and 25 g of protein for 130 calories.
- **Starbucks:** Egg White & Roasted Red Pepper Egg Bites have 8 g of fat. The Bacon & Gruyère Egg Bites have 20 g. See our [Starbucks guide](/chains/starbucks).
- **Chipotle:** the High Protein - Low Calorie Bowl has 29 g of fat in 460 calories. Most of that comes from the guacamole. Avocados are a source of monounsaturated fat, according to the NHS.
- **Sweetgreen:** the Cali Chicken Club Wrap has 90 g of fat and 1,285 calories. That's more than the top of the range for someone eating 2,000 calories a day.

One high-fat meal isn't a disaster. Just balance the rest of your day around it.

## How much fat do you eat on keto?

Far more than usual. According to Harvard's Nutrition Source, popular keto resources suggest about 70% to 80% of calories from fat. That's well above the 20% to 35% range in the US guidelines.

If you're curious, our [keto calculator](/keto-calculator) shows what those numbers look like for you. It's a big change. So it's worth talking it over with your doctor first, especially if you have a health condition or take medicines.

## How can you choose better fats?

Cook with plant oils, eat fish, and snack on nuts. You don't need to count every gram. A few swaps from the WHO, the NHS, and the Dietary Guidelines make a big difference:

1. **Bake, grill, roast, or stir-fry** instead of deep-frying.
2. **Cook with plant oils** like olive, canola, sunflower, or soybean oil more often than butter, lard, or ghee.
3. **Trim visible fat** from meat, and choose leaner cuts.
4. **Eat fish** a couple of times a week.
5. **Snack on nuts and seeds** instead of pastries or cookies.

You'll see some disagreement on dairy. The WHO suggests reduced-fat dairy. The 2025 to 2030 US guidelines encourage full-fat dairy with no added sugar. Both still cap saturated fat at 10% of calories, so that limit is your anchor either way.

### How do you read fat on a label?

The NHS gives simple label rules per 100 g:

- **High fat:** more than 17.5 g. **Low fat:** 3 g or less.
- **High saturated fat:** more than 5 g. **Low saturated fat:** 1.5 g or less.

One warning from the NHS: "lower fat" foods aren't always lower in calories. Sometimes sugar replaces the fat.

<Callout title="When to check with a professional">
If you have high cholesterol, heart disease, diabetes, or a digestive or liver condition, your fat needs may be different. Talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before making big changes.
</Callout>

## What does this mean for you?

Find your gram range, then focus on the type. Based on what I've seen in the research, that's the most useful habit.

Most of your fat should come from fish, nuts, seeds, avocados, and plant oils. Keep saturated fat under 10% of your calories.

## The bottom line

For most adults, 20% to 35% of calories should come from fat. That's roughly 44 to 78 g on a 2,000-calorie day. Keep saturated fat to about 10% of calories, or the NHS limits of 20 g for women and 30 g for men.
