## The male formulas

This page starts with the male setting, so you see the men's versions of four classic formulas. Each one starts with a weight for a man who is 5 feet (152 cm) tall and adds a set amount for each extra inch (2.54 cm):

- **Devine (1974):** 50 kg plus 2.3 kg per inch
- **Robinson (1983):** 52 kg plus 1.9 kg per inch
- **Miller (1983):** 56.2 kg plus 1.41 kg per inch
- **Hamwi (1964):** 48 kg plus 2.7 kg per inch

For a man who is 5 ft 10 in (178 cm), they land between about 155 and 165 lb (70.3 to 75 kg). Notice how Hamwi adds about 6 lb for every inch, while Miller adds only about 3 lb. That is why the formulas spread further apart for taller men.

These formulas were built to help doctors dose medicines, not to set a goal weight.

## Why muscle throws them off

The formulas only know your height. So do BMI-based ranges. A man who lifts can sit well above his "ideal" number while carrying little fat. If that sounds like you, other checks tell you more:

- Our [body fat calculator for men](/body-fat-calculator/for-men) estimates fat with a tape measure.
- Our [FFMI calculator](/ffmi-calculator) shows how much muscle you carry for your height.
- A waist under half your height is a simple target that works even for muscular people, according to NICE.

## A note on age

Adult BMI cut-offs don't change with age. The CDC uses the same ones for all adults. Still, for men 65 and older, a 2014 review found the overweight range was not linked to a higher risk of death, while a BMI under 23 was. Keeping muscle matters more as you age.
